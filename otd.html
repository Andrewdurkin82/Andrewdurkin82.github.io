18-Minute OTD

When Pizza Hut changed the scoring target, I used AI to analyze prior performance, look for timing patterns, run delivery and dispatch scenarios, and identify the workflow milestones that actually controlled the outcome.

The problem

A new metric made the old workflow obsolete.

The new target focused on getting delivery orders out the door within 18 minutes. Under stress, the store could exceed 60-minute service times and was consistently ranked last among 30 stores.

The obvious response would have been “move faster.” That is not a process design. I used AI as an analysis layer to break the operation apart: which decisions controlled the clock, when they happened, what patterns showed up in prior performance, and what changed when different dispatch choices were simulated. That gave me specific hypotheses to test in the actual store instead of another motivational speech about urgency.

AI analysis layer

Use the model to interrogate the operation.

I used AI to work through historical performance data and operating observations, looking for patterns that were difficult to see one shift at a time.

The useful part was not asking AI for a generic “better delivery process.” I used it to compare timing patterns, pressure-test assumptions, and run what-if scenarios around oven timing, driver availability, DoorDash handoff, doubles, and dispatch choices.

Those simulations helped turn a single 18-minute score into a sequence of testable milestones. AI could generate and compare scenarios quickly; the real store then told me which ideas survived contact with reality.

The redesign

Break the work into decision points.

01

Work delivery orders immediately

Delivery orders could not sit behind unrelated work if the 18-minute clock was going to matter operationally.

02

Target the oven early

Use roughly minute 6 as the target for entering an approximately 8-minute cook cycle, leaving room for cut, box, dispatch, and handoff.

03

Protect delivery flow during rushes

Use a separate lower oven queue for delivery when needed so heavy carryout volume does not consume the delivery clock.

04

Make the driver vs. DoorDash decision while the order cooks

Instead of waiting until the end, decide whether a driver can realistically leave before the 18-minute target while there is still time to adapt.

05

Reassess doubles at dispatch

Do not lock in doubles too early. Use the actual state of driver availability, geography, and ready times when the order is ready to leave.

The clock controls cooking. Dispatch adapts.

The workflow separates what must happen on a predictable timeline from what should stay flexible until the latest useful decision point.

Simulation + capacity

Model driver-minutes, not just mileage.

One of the useful simulation questions was whether two geographically close orders were actually a good double. The better resource to model was driver availability over time, not just miles on a map.

40 min

Two separate 10-minute outbound / 10-minute return runs consume roughly forty total driver-minutes.

27 min

A structured double with 10 minutes to stop one, 7 minutes between stops, and 10 minutes back consumes about twenty-seven.

That scenario saves 13 driver-minutes. I used comparisons like this to test dispatch logic before treating it as a rule. The point was not “always double.” It was to reason about total capacity instead of relying on a vague sense that two addresses looked nearby.

Critical milestone

Four minutes in the oven became an important signal.

Analysis

A hidden milestone emerged

Pattern analysis, scenario work, and live operating observation pointed to four minutes into the oven cycle as a useful decision point for whether an order was still on pace for the new target.

Adoption

The milestone entered the working process

The team began using the timing insight operationally rather than treating OTD as one undifferentiated countdown.

Later alignment

Corporate scoring moved in the same direction

Pizza Hut later began tracking four-minute oven time as a major performance metric, independently reinforcing the importance of the same stage in the workflow.

Outcome

The store moved from consistently ranking last out of 30 stores to typically landing between 5th and 10th in performance metrics.

Service performance improved substantially, and sales increased during the period after the workflow changes. I treat those as related operational outcomes, not proof that one process change alone caused the sales growth.

Why it belongs in an AI portfolio

AI was the analysis layer, not the thing delivering the pizza.

I used AI to analyze prior data, search for patterns, run operational scenarios, challenge assumptions, and model the decision points before testing them in the real workflow. The value was not replacing human judgment. It was expanding how quickly I could examine the system, compare alternatives, and turn messy operating experience into something testable.
